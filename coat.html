Coat for Esme: Designing for a Real Person

01 · The Problem

Build an ideal coat for my teammate Esme, based entirely on understanding her specific needs, wants, and aspirations rather than assumptions.

Her needs: Extreme warmth for 15-minute walks. Deep center pockets for hands, phone, keys, and wallet (side pockets felt awkward). A hood that does not catch on her backpack. A high collar that sits above the collarbone so she does not need a scarf. One coat with no layering. A quality zipper.

Her wants: A red coat (her favorite color, which she does not own yet; she already has blue and black). Matte finish. Monochrome, not two colors. Quirky details: asymmetrical zipper, thumb loops. Clean design with no ribbons, thin puff lines, ruching, or fussy elements. Detachable hood.

Her aspirations: Get compliments the way her unique blue Aritzia coat does. Express freedom after 13 years in school uniform. Stand out with less typical colors and details. Feel ready and confident for busy student days. Navigate NYC winters fashionably. Quality that lasts.

"I'm not gonna wear a sweater and then a jacket and then a coat."
02 · My Role and Collaboration

I conducted primary and secondary research to understand Esme's needs and wants. I interviewed her directly, then designed and iterated based on what she told me. The sketching and annotation were done by me, with Esme as the design subject and feedback source.

03 · Iterations and How I Reached the Design

Sketch 1: A basic puffer silhouette establishing the high collar, large puffer sections, and thumb loops.

Sketch 2: Added the detachable hood with a structural collar to solve the backpack-catching problem, plus visible button closure detail.

Final colored design: Introduced the full red colorway with darker red hardware, asymmetrically placed zipper offset to the side, large zippered center pockets, and darker red thumb loops with lighter red contrast stitching. The hood is reversible with darker and lighter red sides.

04 · How the Design Solved Her Needs

Detachable hood with reversible darker/lighter red sides: solves the backpack-catching issue while offering monochrome styling options.

High collar with top button above the collarbone: eliminates the need for a scarf, keeps her warm, and looks fashionable while staying minimal.

Asymmetrically placed zipper offset to the side with darker red hardware: adds the quirky detail she loves, maintains the monochrome aesthetic, and uses a zipper rather than buttons.

Large zippered pockets positioned toward the center with darker red hardware: keeps hands and items secure while looking fashionable.

Darker red thumb loops with lighter red contrast stitching: keeps hands warm, looks fashionable, and maintains the monochrome design.

Good-length matte red puffer with thick insulation: her favorite color she does not have yet, warmth prioritized for extreme cold, clean design avoiding small details, minimal monochrome aesthetic that is fashionable yet functional.

Sensory design: The quality zipper and puffer material create a satisfying swoosh sound. Zipping up signals "I'm ready for the day." Unzipping signals "I'm done and can relax." The hood buttons make a clicking sound so she knows when it is secured.

05 · Challenges and Designs Not Pursued

A two-color coat was considered but Esme specifically said blue and red "feels like an American flag." Monochrome was non-negotiable. Button-front closure was explored but she preferred zippers. Thin puff lines were visually tempting but she explicitly wanted larger puffer sections. Every eliminated option came from listening more carefully to what she said.

06 · How It Affected Esme

The design solves every friction point in her current winter wardrobe: no scarf layering, no backpack snagging, no cold hands, no extra layers. It gives her the red coat she does not own, the monochrome aesthetic she wants, the quirky details she values, and a quality that matches brands she trusts like Aritzia.

07 · What I Learned

Primary and secondary research surfaces needs that users themselves cannot always articulate. The sensory dimension of the zipper sound as a psychological transition ritual was not something Esme named directly. It emerged from listening for emotional patterns beneath functional requests. Good design solves what people say and what they mean.
