Project Rivas: Flood Emergency Protocol for Alphabet City

Team: Aran · Esme · Krysten · Nandini

01 · The Problem

Many residents in flood-prone neighborhoods like Alphabet City, including seniors, children, and non-English speakers, are disconnected from existing disaster resources, leaving them without clear direction before, during, and after a flood.

Renters in older Alphabet City residential buildings, especially those on ground and basement floors, are particularly vulnerable. Institutional systems exist but do not reach everyone, leaving people without real-time information in their language or trusted local channels when a crisis hits.

02 · The Insight

The Heimlich maneuver poster has saved lives for decades, not because people read it in a crisis, but because they absorbed it through daily exposure. Rivas applies the same logic: simple, reinforced visuals embedded into everyday life so that when a flood hits, residents already know what to do.

"The only thing that functions well in a disaster is common sense." G. Bonati, 69, Alphabet City since 1981
03 · The Solution

Rivas Flood Protocol is a poster designed for apartment lobbies in vulnerable areas. It has three components:

Multilingual visual instructions in English, Spanish, Chinese, French, and Ukrainian, covering 9 steps across three phases: before the storm, during flooding, and after the flood. Icon-based so no reading is required to follow the core sequence.

Updatable shelter information on the wall. The poster has a slot where a printed insert can be placed and swapped out before a predicted storm. HPD issues a new insert with the current nearest shelter addresses, dated and signed by the building's designated contact. This keeps shelter information accurate without replacing the whole poster, and it works entirely without power or internet.

QR code for real-time updates linking to a live shelter map and neighborhood group chat. Residents without a smartphone can text their zip code to get the same information via SMS.

04 · Target Users and Stakeholders

Target users: Residents of apartment buildings in flood-risk zones, especially non-English speakers, elderly residents, people without smartphones, and children.

Key stakeholders: NYC Emergency Management (NYCEM), which distributes official evacuation zone data. NYC Department of Housing Preservation and Development (HPD), which could mandate poster installation as a building code requirement. Building owners and co-op boards, who maintain lobby installations. Tenants, who are the primary users and community advocates.

05 · How Rivas Ensures Fairness

The five languages were chosen to reflect the primary languages spoken across Alphabet City's communities. The poster requires no app, no wifi, and no power, so residents without smartphones or tech familiarity are never excluded. It is an open resource available to all residential buildings regardless of income, tenure, or immigration status.

06 · Challenges and Designs Not Pursued

Early concepts focused on digital-only solutions such as apps and push notifications, but primary research with Sandy survivors showed this would exclude the most vulnerable people. Building-wide intercom systems and door-to-door leaflets were explored but required ongoing resources most buildings do not have. The poster with a swappable shelter insert was the solution that could be installed once and stay relevant over time.

07 · What Residents Experienced During Sandy

Residents were unaware of risks before the storm. Repeated false-alarm warnings had made people dismissive. When the real storm arrived, phones went dead, there was no running water, no posted instructions anywhere, and non-English speakers had no recourse. People contacted officials and received auto-replies. They returned home without guidance, damage went undocumented, and the cycle reset with no lasting change.

"If there was some guidance like that, that was ubiquitous, I think that would certainly be helpful." A. Nayar, 39, Chinatown since 2007
08 · What I Learned

Designing for the most constrained users produced a solution that works better for everyone. The gap between institutional emergency systems and the communities they are meant to serve is often not a resource gap. It is a translation gap. Good design is translation.
